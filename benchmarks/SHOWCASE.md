## The scene

`benchmarks/scenes/showcase-gallery.json`, generated by
`scripts/make-showcase-scene.fsx`. **22,106,877 instanced triangles** across 49
objects - roughly 370x the heaviest of the original benchmark scenes
(roman-bust, 59,944).

| Model | Triangles | Source |
|---|---:|---|
| Thai Statuette | 10,000,000 | Stanford Large Geometric Models Archive |
| XYZ RGB Dragon | 7,219,045 | Stanford Large Geometric Models Archive |
| Happy Buddha | 1,087,716 | Stanford 3D Scanning Repository |
| Stanford Dragon | 871,414 | Stanford 3D Scanning Repository |
| Armadillo | 345,944 x2 | Stanford 3D Scanning Repository |
| Horse | 96,966 x10 | already in repository |
| Bunny | 69,451 x18 | Stanford 3D Scanning Repository |
| Sphere (glass / chrome) | 1,216 x14 | generated, closed |

Two rectangular area lights, a point rim light and a sky environment. Rough
metal, rough dielectric, mirror and glass. Depth 8.

Placement is derived from each mesh's real bounds through the project's own PLY
parser, not hand-tuned constants, so the layout survives an asset changing.

## CPU: path traced with global illumination

1600x1000, 196 spp, depth 8, multi-jittered, denoised.

| Phase | Time |
|---|---:|
| Scene load (PLY parsing) | 73.7 s |
| **BVH build over 22.1M triangles** | **0.06 s** |
| Trace | **28.0 min** |
| Encode | 0.5 s |
| Peak RSS | 12.0 GB |

The BVH figure is the one worth pausing on: the binned-SAH builder handles 22.1
million triangles in **sixty milliseconds**, while parsing the ASCII PLY files
takes over a minute. Acceleration-structure construction is nowhere near being a
bottleneck at this scale.

## GPU: Whitted, FP32

`benchmarks/scenes/showcase-gallery-flat.json` - the CUDA worker requires
authored vertex normals for smooth meshes and the Stanford scans carry none, so
the GPU variant is flat shaded. At these triangle densities the faceting is not
visible.

1600x1000, 4 camera samples, 4 light samples, depth 4.

| Phase | Time |
|---|---:|
| Scene load | 8.9 s |
| BVH build (world-space, all 22.1M triangles) | 90.0 s |
| Kernel compile | 3.7 s |
| Upload | 0.4 s |
| Trace | **3.21 s** |
| Peak device memory | **5.18 GB** of 8 GB |

22.1M triangles fit in device memory only because FP32 halved every structure -
at FP64 this scene would not have fitted at all.

### These two are NOT comparable

The CPU render is a **path tracer with global illumination at 196 spp**; the GPU
render is a **Whitted renderer with no indirect light at 4 spp**. Different
algorithms, different sample counts, different images. Compare them visually -
the CPU image has soft contact shadows, colour bleeding and light wrapping into
crevices that the GPU image simply does not compute - but do not divide the two
numbers.

The GPU speedups that *are* measured like-for-like are in
[PHASE2-RESULTS.md](PHASE2-RESULTS.md): 5.53x on identical work.

## What the image shows

- **Global illumination.** The floor under and between the statues picks up
  colour from them; crevices in the statuette fill with bounced light rather
  than going black.
- **Energy-conserving rough metal.** The gold dragon and copper dragon keep
  their tint at grazing angles instead of darkening - that is the
  multiple-scattering compensation, without which a roughness-1 metal loses 62%
  of its energy.
- **Refraction with total internal reflection.** The glass spheres invert the
  scene behind them and show the horizon bending through.
- **Denoising.** 196 spp with the OIDN pass, guided by albedo and normal buffers
  captured at the first non-specular hit so the glass and chrome stay sharp.

# San Miguel at 4K, on the GPU

![san miguel](../artifacts/sanmiguel/san-miguel-4K-gpu-fp32.png)

`artifacts/sanmiguel/san-miguel-4K-gpu-fp32.png`

San Miguel is the standard heavy architectural scene in rendering research - a
Mexican hacienda courtyard with an arcade, fountain, cafe furniture and dense
foliage. It is the closest freely-redistributable thing to a production set.

| | |
|---|---|
| Source | `casual-effects.com` research model archive, 536 MB |
| Geometry | 1.1 GB OBJ, **9,980,699 triangles**, 287 material groups |
| Textures | 264 diffuse maps (323 PNGs shipped) |

## Getting it in

The renderer reads PLY; the research scenes ship as OBJ. `scripts/obj-to-ply.fsx`
converts one into a binary PLY per material group plus a SceneFormat scene,
because the scene format carries one material per object. It transfers positions,
normals, texture coordinates, OBJ diffuse/specular and `map_Kd`.

```sh
dotnet fsi scripts/obj-to-ply.fsx -- <model.obj> <outputDir> <sceneId>
```

The 1.1 GB OBJ parses in **11 seconds**.

## 4K result

3840x2160, 64 camera samples, 4 light samples, depth 4, FP32.

| Phase | Time |
|---|---:|
| Scene load (287 PLY meshes + 264 textures) | 11.1 s |
| BVH build over 9.98M triangles | 47.1 s |
| Kernel JIT | 3.6 s |
| Upload | 0.88 s |
| **Trace** | **80.07 s** |
| Download | 0.08 s |
| Encode (4K PNG) | 3.22 s |
| **Total** | **146.2 s** |
| Peak device memory | 3.45 GB of 8 GB |

**530,841,600 camera samples in 80.07 s - 6.63 M camera samples per second**,
each traversing a 9.98M-triangle BVH and casting shadow rays on top.

For scale: the same GPU, before this work, rendered a **21,072-triangle** scene
at 1024x1024/64spp in 145.7 seconds. It now renders a 9.98M-triangle scene at
4K/64spp in 80 seconds - about 8x the pixels, 470x the geometry, in half the
time.

## Honest limitations

- **The GPU path is Whitted, not a path tracer.** There is no indirect bounce.
  A real arcade is lit largely by light bouncing off its own walls, so the
  ambient and sky terms are standing in for that. This is why the courtyard
  reads as shaded rather than sunlit, and why the material ambient coefficient
  had to be raised from 0.04 to 0.35 - at 0.04 the arcade rendered black.
- **Diffuse textures only.** Normal, specular and displacement maps in the MTL
  are not transferred.
- **No alpha test**, so one alpha-mapped cut-out material renders as solid
  geometry.
- The CPU path tracer would render this scene with correct bounce light, but at
  4K it would take hours rather than 80 seconds.

## Why not Moana

Disney's Moana Island Scene is the obvious "Pixar-grade" target and it is freely
available - but it ships as 15-131 GB of PBRT and Ptex data, and pbrt-v4 needs
about 29 GB of VRAM to render it against the 8 GB available here. It would need
an out-of-core geometry and texture pipeline that this renderer does not have.
San Miguel is the largest production-style scene that fits.

# Making it look photographic

The first San Miguel render read as early-2000s game graphics. That was not a
limit of the geometry - it was four specific, nameable defects.

| Cause | Why it reads as CG | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **No global illumination** (GPU is Whitted) plus a constant ambient fill | A uniform ambient added everywhere is the pre-2005 lighting model. It flattens form, erases contact shadows and removes depth. | Render on the **CPU path tracer**, and delete the ambient term entirely - indirect bounce does that work properly |
| **Hard highlight clipping** - `min 1.0` then gamma | Everything above white lands on exactly white, so sunlit surfaces become flat patches with no detail. Film and cameras roll off instead. | **ACES filmic tone mapping** (`"transfer": "aces"`) |
| Nearest-neighbour texture sampling | Blocky, aliased texels | **None needed** - jittered camera rays turn nearest sampling into stochastic box filtering, so it converges away with sample count. Only magnified textures would still show it. |
| Hard directional shadows | No penumbra | Partly addressed: sky-dominant lighting gives soft ambient occlusion through GI |

There turned out to be a fifth, found only by measuring: **no exposure
control**. San Miguel's median pixel is linear 0.0067 - roughly two orders of
magnitude below display white - because it is a high-dynamic-range scene shot
into shade. Without an exposure stage the only ways to fix that are to distort
the lighting or to post-process outside the renderer. `RenderOptions.Exposure`
and `--exposure` now apply a linear multiplier before the display transfer, the
way a camera does. The final image uses **exposure 8**; at 16 the sunlit foliage
blows out.

These were all cheap: a command-line flag on a renderer we already had, about
twenty lines of tone mapping, and a multiply.

## Re-tonemapping without re-rendering

Renders retain unclamped linear PFM alongside the PNG, so exposure and tone
mapping can be revisited **without re-tracing**:

```sh
dotnet fsi scripts/tonemap-pfm.fsx -- <in.pfm> <out.png> <exposure> [transfer]
```

It uses the renderer's own `Colour.ToDisplayColor`, so the result is identical
to having rendered with those settings. The exposure for the final image was
chosen this way rather than by repeating a 63-minute render five times.

## ACES filmic transfer

Added to `Colour.ToDisplayColor` alongside `gamma2`, `srgb` and `linear`.
Narkowicz's 2015 approximation of the ACES curve, then sRGB encoding:

```
x -> (x(2.51x + 0.03)) / (x(2.43x + 0.59) + 0.14)
```

The other transfers hard-clip. This compresses the highlight shoulder and adds a
slight toe, so a sunlit wall keeps its texture instead of blowing to white.

## Two scene variants, deliberately

- `san-miguel.json` - **Whitted/GPU**. Material ambient 0.35, scene ambient
  0.85. The fake fill is mandatory here: at the OBJ's native 0.04 the arcade
  renders black, because without indirect bounce nothing else lights it.
- `san-miguel-pt.json` - **path traced/CPU**. Ambient **0.0** everywhere, sun
  6.5, sky 2.6, depth 8. The ambient term is not merely unnecessary, it would
  wash the image out - GI already carries that light, and adding a constant on
  top double-counts it.

That two-variant split is the honest consequence of the GPU still being a
Whitted renderer. Porting the path tracer to the GPU would collapse them into
one, and is the largest piece of work still outstanding.
